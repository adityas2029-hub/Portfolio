Dating, designed for neurodivergent minds.

A dating product built around sensory load, communication preferences and consent — not just swipes. Research-led, made with the people it’s for, not just at them.

COVER · drop in hero shot
§ 01

Context

Existing dating apps were built around a neurotypical assumption: that flirting is improvised, signals are read implicitly, and intensity is fun. For a sizeable slice of users that’s exhausting at best and unsafe at worst.

The founders had lived experience and a thesis — that the same product, designed differently, could serve everyone better. My job was to turn that thesis into something shippable.

We started with 30 conversations with autistic and ADHD adults across three cities. The pattern wasn’t accessibility issues, it was emotional friction: scripts for openers, surprise voice notes, the dread of meeting in a loud bar.

“I want to date. I don’t want to read minds at the same time.”
§ 02

The problem

Reframe dating as a sequence of consented, low-surprise steps — without making it feel clinical, or like a separate “special” app.

  • Match outcomes had to stay strong enough to compete with mainstream apps on day one.
  • No vocabulary that othered users — “sensory mode” not “disability mode”.
  • Defaults had to do the work; toggling preferences couldn’t be the product.
§ 03

Process

Designed in the open with a panel of 12 users. Every feature shipped to them in a TestFlight a week before the wider beta.

Three principles I went back to constantly: name the next step, let people opt in to surprise, consent is a UI element, not a checkbox. Every screen got tested against those.

§ 05

Reflections

The hardest thing was holding the line on defaults. Every stakeholder meeting wanted to add a setting; every user session asked for one less. The compromise was a quiet onboarding that read your answers and silently calibrated the experience. It’s not perfect. The next version needs a way to recalibrate over time without asking again.
