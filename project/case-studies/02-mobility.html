How a city decides to move.

Re-routing the way a 12-million-person city chooses between transit, ride-share and personal mobility. A platform problem dressed up as a UI.

COVER · drop in city / map shot
§ 01

Context

A municipal authority wanted residents to choose lower-emission journeys without making it feel like homework. The existing tools were five separate apps, none of which talked to each other.

The mandate sounded like a UI consolidation. After a week of field work it was clear it wasn’t — it was a service redesign with a UI on the surface.

“I don’t need an app. I need to get my kid to school by 8.”
§ 02

The problem

Make “the right choice” the easy choice — without lecturing, without locking residents out of fast options when they’re needed.

  • Six separate ticketing systems with different identity, fares and accessibility flags.
  • No shared idea of “a journey” — each operator owned only its leg.
  • Civic UI had to read as trustworthy, not as a slick consumer app pretending to be neutral.
§ 03

Process

Started with a service blueprint covering the full journey — including stops, transfers, payment, missed connections and the moments people stop trusting public transit.

Designed a shared journey object the city, operators and the app could all read. The UI then became a thin layer of choices on top of it. Three rounds of co-design with commuters of different ages and abilities.

§ 05

Reflections

The thing I underestimated was governance. A shared journey object only works if six operators agree to update it on time, every time. The next version needs less elegant data modelling and more political design — a stronger SLA layer and clearer accountability when the data is wrong.
